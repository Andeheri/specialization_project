\section{Current controller}
\label{sec:controller_design_current_controller}

To help fascilitate the design of the position and torque controllers, we will first design a controller for the armature current $I_a$ with the armature voltage $E_a$. This will allow us to treat the armature current as a control input in the position control design, simplifying the dynamics significantly. As the dynamics of the armature current is so fast, the controller has to be as cheap as possible to compute. Hence, it will be designed as a simple proportional (P) controller, but with a feedforward (FF) term to account for the back EMF. Let's first consider the electrical dynamics of the motor from \cref{eq:motor_model_electrical_rewrite}:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_electrical_dynamics}
    L_a \frac{\mathrm{d}I_a}{\mathrm{d}t} + R_a I_a + K_b \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t}= E_a.
\end{equation}

Given the time varying reference current $r(t)$, we can design the input voltage $E_a(t)$ as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_voltage_input}
    E_a(t) = \underbrace{K_b \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}}_{\text{Feedforward term}} + \;\underbrace{K_p \left(r(t) - I_a(t)\right)}_{\text{P controller}}.
\end{equation}

Substituting \cref{eq:controller_design_voltage_input} into \cref{eq:controller_design_electrical_dynamics}, we get:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_current_dynamics}
    L_a \frac{\mathrm{d}I_a(t)}{\mathrm{d}t} + R_a I_a(t) = K_p (r(t) - I_a(t)) \triangleq u(t).
\end{equation}

This controller is supposed to be ran in real time on a microcontroller. That way it won't have access to continuous time signals, but rather discrete time signals. Hence, we will need to discretize the controller for stability analysis and tuning. For this analysis we will assume that the dynamics of the motor are slow enough such that the feedforward term can be treated as a constant. This is a reasonable assumption since the feedforward term is based on the angular velocity, which changes relatively slowly compared to the armature current.

\subsection{Discretization of current controller}
Suppose the controller is ran with a update period of $T_s$. Assuming that the input voltage $E_a$ is held constant between sampling times, i.e.,

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_discretized_input}
    u(t)=u[k] = K_p (r[k] - I_a[k]), \quad k T_s \leq t < (k+1)T_s,
\end{equation}

the dynamics in \cref{eq:controller_design_current_dynamics} then becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_current_interval_dynamics}
    L_a \frac{\mathrm{d}I_a(\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau} + R_a I_a(\tau) = u[k], \quad \tau = t - k T_s \in [0, T_s].
\end{equation}

To solve it, let's multiply by the integrating factor $e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} \tau}$, which gives:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_current_integrating_factor}
    e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} \tau} \left[L_a \frac{\mathrm{d}I_a(\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau} + R_a I_a(\tau)\right] = u[k] e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} \tau}.
\end{equation}

Notice how this can be rewritten as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_current_integrating_factor_rewrite}
    L_a \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau} \left(e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} \tau} I_a(\tau)\right) = u[k] e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} \tau}.
\end{equation}

Integrating both sides of \cref{eq:controller_design_current_integrating_factor_rewrite} with respect to $\tau$, over the entire interval from $0$ to $T_s$, we get the discretized dynamics of the current controller:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_discretized}
    \begin{aligned}
        \left[e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} \tau} I_a(\tau)\right]_{\tau = 0}^{\tau = T_s} &= \frac{u[k]}{L_a} \int_0^{T_s} e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} \tau} \mathrm{d}\tau,\\
        \implies e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} T_s} \underbrace{I_a(T_s)}_{I_a[k+1]} - \underbrace{I_a(0)}_{I_a[k]} &= u[k] \frac{e^{\frac{R_a}{L_a} T_s} - 1}{R_a},\\
        \implies I_a[k+1] &= \underbrace{e^{-\frac{R_a}{L_a} T_s}}_{\triangleq a} I_a[k] + u[k] \underbrace{\frac{1 - e^{-\frac{R_a}{L_a} T_s}}{R_a}}_{\triangleq b},\\
        \implies I_a[k+1] &= a I_a[k] + b u[k].
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Substituting in $u[k]$ from \cref{eq:controller_design_discretized_input}, we get:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_discretized_reference}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_a[k+1] &= a I_a[k] + b K_p (r[k] - I_a[k]),\\
        \implies I_a[k+1] &= \left(a - b K_p\right) I_a[k] + b K_p r[k].
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}



\subsection{Stability analysis and tuning of controller}

To guarantee stability of the current controller, we can look at the z-transform of the system. Taking the z-transform of \cref{eq:controller_design_discretized_reference}, disregarding any initial conditions, we get:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_current_dynamics_z_transform}
    \begin{aligned}
        \left[z - (a - b K_p)\right] I_a(z) &= b K_p r(z),\\
        \implies \frac{I_a(z)}{r(z)} &= \frac{b K_p}{z - (a - b K_p)}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Clearly, \cref{eq:controller_design_current_dynamics_z_transform} has a pole at $z = a - b K_p$. As discussed in \cref{subsec:theory_discrete_time_system_analysis}, for the system to be stable, we need the pole to be inside the unit circle. In other words, we need:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_current_stability_condition}
    \begin{aligned}
        |a - b K_p| &< 1 \implies \left|e^{-\frac{R_a}{L_a} T_s} - K_p \frac{1 - e^{-\frac{R_a}{L_a} T_s}}{R_a}\right| < 1,\\
        \implies K_p &< R_a \frac{1 + e^{-\frac{R_a}{L_a} T_s}}{1 - e^{-\frac{R_a}{L_a} T_s}} \triangleq K_{p,\text{max}}(T_s).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

One downside to using a P controller is that there will be a steady-state error. To eliminate this, we can artifically give the controller a higher reference current. To do this we will need to know the steady-state value of the current for a given reference. Assuming we apply a step reference of $r[k] = r_0$ for all $k \geq 0$. Provided we choose a $K_p$ that satisfies \cref{eq:controller_design_current_stability_condition}, the recursion will converge, and as we let $t \to \infty \implies k \to \infty$, \cref{eq:controller_design_discretized_reference} becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_current_steady_state}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_a[\infty] &= (a - b K_p) I_a[\infty] + b K_p r_0,\\
        \implies I_a[\infty] &= r_0 \frac{b K_p}{1 - a + b K_p} = r_0 \frac{\frac{1 - a}{R_a} K_p}{1 - a + \frac{1 - a}{R_a} K_p} = r_0 \frac{K_p}{R_a + K_p}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Hence to eliminate the steady-state error, we can choose the reference to be:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_current_reference_tuning}
    r_1 = \frac{R_a + K_p}{K_p} r_0 \implies I_a[\infty] = r_1 \frac{K_p}{R_a + K_p} = r_0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Evaluation of controller}

When the motor is starting from rest, both the P+FF and P controllers are able to reach the target current fast. However, as the motor picks up speed, the back-EMF becomes significant, and the P controller is no longer able to fully compensate for the induced voltage, and the current starts to deviate from the reference current, as can be seen in \cref{fig:controller_design_current_controller_simulation_p_no_speed}. The P+FF controller on the other hand continues to perform well, as it can account for the back-EMF, as shown in \cref{fig:controller_design_current_controller_simulation_pff_no_speed}. Note that the observed effect of the increasing back-EMF could be more or less significant in the actual system, since the motor inertia $J_m$ is only based on similar motors, and not from the actual motor. If the true inertia is smaller, the observed effect would be much greater. When the motor is already spinning at a high speeds, the differences between the two controllers becomes even more significant. At no point does the P controller reach its target current, as can be seen in \cref{fig:controller_design_current_controller_simulation_p_with_speed}. The P+FF controller on the other hand continues to perform well, as it can account for the back-EMF, as shown in \cref{fig:controller_design_current_controller_simulation_pff_with_speed}.

As can be seen in \cref{fig:controller_design_current_controller_simulation_noise_0.2ms} and \cref{fig:controller_design_current_controller_simulation_noise_1ms}, the added noise to the current measurements doesn't really affect the stability of the controller. The current oscillates slightly when there's noise present, compared to without. When considering the motor inertia to be smaller, shown in \cref{fig:controller_design_current_controller_simulation_small_Jm}, a longer update period of $T_s = \SI{1.0}{\milli\second}$ results in the current getting a slight steady-state-error, which is not present with the shorter update period of $T_s = \SI{0.2}{\milli\second}$. This is likely due to the assumption of constant velocity between samples being less accurate for longer sampling times. This causes the feedforward term to be less accurate, which in turn causes a steady-state error. With a shorter update period, the assumption of a constant speed is more valid, which allows the feedforward term to be more accurate, which in turn enables the controller to track the reference current better. 

Overall, the P+FF controller shows promising results for controlling the armature current in our system. Due to the saturation in the voltage of $E_a=\pm\SI{12}{\volt}$, there is a maximum rate of change for the current. During simulation it was found to be $\dot{I_a}_{\jnt{\text{max}}}\approx\SI{2.0}{\kilo\ampere\per\second}$, when the motor was starting from still, which is more or less instantaneous seen from the dynamics of the mechanical system. Hence, we will treat the armature current as a control input for further control design.

\section{Torque control}
\label{sec:controller_design_torque_control}

When the hand interacts with objects, trying to control the position of the hand becomes irrelevant, as the hand will be constrained by the object. Instead, we want to control the torque generated by the motor, which in turn will allow us to control the force applied to the object.


When grasping an object, assuming the object is rigid and that the cable is inelastic, the angular velocity and acceleration of the motor will be $\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} \equiv 0$ and $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\theta}{\mathrm{d}t^2} \equiv 0$, respectively. Based on \cref{eq:motor_model_mechanical_rewrite}, this results in the following relation:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:motor_torque_equal_load_torque}
    \tau_m = K_t I_a = \tau_l.
\end{equation}

Hence, all the torque generated by the motor, which is proportional to the current, would be applied to the load. This means the applied torque can be controlled directly through the current. In \cref{sec:controller_design_current_controller} we designed a controller for the armature current $I_a$ through the input armature voltage $E_a$. With $\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} \equiv 0$, the back-EMF will be $K_b \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} \equiv 0$. Therefore we can also simplify the current controller in \cref{eq:controller_design_voltage_input} to just a P controller, as there is no back-EMF to account for. This will reduce the computational load on the microcontroller. For a given reference torque $r_{\jnt{\tau_m}}$, we can calculate the reference current $r_{\jnt{I_a}}$ as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_torque_control_reference}
    r_{\jnt{I_a}} = \frac{r_{\jnt{\tau_m}}}{K_t}.
\end{equation}

If it's more convenient to control the force applied to the object instead of the torque, we can easily convert between the two using the relation $\tau_m = F_m r_{\jnt{spindle}}$, where $r_{\jnt{spindle}}$ is the radius of the spindle mounted on the motor. Hence, for a given reference force $r_{\jnt{F}}$, we can calculate the reference current $r_{\jnt{I_a}}$ as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_force_control_reference}
    r_{\jnt{I_a}} = r_{\jnt{F}} \frac{r_{\jnt{spindle}}}{K_t}.
\end{equation}

Figure \cref{fig:controller_design_torque_control_rigid_object} shows emulated scenarios of the finger grasping a rigid object, assuming no elasticity in the cable, and no friction between the motor and the finger. The motor is supplied with $\SI{3}{\volt}$ for the first $\SI{5}{\milli\second}$. Then at $t=\SI{5}{\milli\second}$, the finger makes contact with the object, and $\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\theta}{\mathrm{d}t^2} \equiv 0$. At the same time, the torque controller is activated, and the reference torque is set to $r_{\jnt{\tau_m}} = \SI{1.5}{\newton\meter}$. For the current controller, the proportional gain is set to $K_p = \frac{1}{2} K_{p, max}$, like in \cref{subsec:current_simulation}. \cref{fig:controller_design_torque_control_rigid_object_0.2ms} and \cref{fig:controller_design_torque_control_rigid_object_1.0ms} show the results for $T_s=\SI{0.2}{\milli\second}$ and $T_s=\SI{1.0}{\milli\second}$, respectively. As can be seen in the figures, the motor torque $\tau_m$ quickly converges to the reference torque $r_{\jnt{\tau_m}}$ for both cases.

Ideally, if there was no friction between the motor and the finger, $\tau_l = \tau_{finger}$. In reality, because of the bowden tube the cable is running through, and other inconsistencies in the setup, there will be some friction. Therefore, we can expect $\tau_l = \tau_{finger} + \tau_{friction}$, where $\tau_{friction}$ is the torque lost to friction. Hence, without any direct measurement of the applied force to the finger, we would expect that the applied force to the finger will be less than the reference force. However, with integrated force sensors in the fingertip, this wouldn't be an issue. 

\subsection{Force control with integrated fingertip force sensors}
\label{subsec:fingertip_sensors}

With integrated force sensors in the fingertip, we could use a cascade control approach. Since the motor torque and force are proportional to the armature current, we could then define the reference current $r_{\jnt{I_a}}$ as a feedforward and PI controller with the reference force $r_{\jnt{F}}$ and the measured force $F_{meas}$ as input:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_torque_control_reference_with_force_feedback}
    r_{\jnt{I_a}}(t) = \underbrace{\frac{r_\jnt{F} r_{\jnt{spindle}}}{K_t}}_{\text{Feedforward}} + \underbrace{K_p (r_{\jnt{F}} - F_{meas}(t)) + K_i \int_{t_0}^t (r_{\jnt{F}} - F_{meas}(\tau)) \, d\tau}_{\text{PI \, controller}}.
\end{equation}

In our system, the cables are running through bowden tubes. This introduces a significant amount of friction. Therefore the original assumption in \cref{eq:motor_torque_equal_load_torque} that all the torque generated by the motor is applied to the load, becomes invalid. \cite{jeongFeedforwardFrictionCompensation2015} proposes the friction from cables going through bowden tubes to be proportional with the applied force. We then get the following relation:

\begin{equation}
    F_{\jnt{finger}} = F_m \left(1 - \exp(-\mu \Phi)\right),
\end{equation}

where $\mu$ is the friction coefficient, and $\Phi$ is the total wrap angle of the cable around the bowden tube. In theory one could include this in the feedforward term, but for the time being, I'll assume it to be unkown. For simulation purposes,  I'll assume that $\exp(-\mu \Phi)\equiv 0.1$. This means that only $90\%$ of the motor force is applied to the finger. \cref{fig:controller_with_vs_without_friction_force_control} shows the simulation of the force control with and without the integrated fingertip force sensors, with $T_s = \SI{0.2}{\milli\second}$ and $r_{\jnt{F}}=\SI{100}{\newton}$. Here the controller parameters were chosen simply through repeated simulation, where $K_p = 0.001$ and $K_i = 8$. These will most likely have to be retuned in the actual system. As can be seen in \cref{fig:force_control_friction_without}, without the integrated fingertip force sensors, the applied force to the finger never reaches its target, as is expected due to the friction. With the integrated fingertip force sensors, as shown in \cref{fig:force_control_friction_with}, the applied force to the finger quickly converges to the reference force, despite the friction.

In the simulation, the the resultant force after the friction was assumed directly applied to the fingertip. In the real system however, a whiffle tree will be used. It might therefore be usefult to scale the feedforward term to account for the total force the motor will have to pull on the whiffle tree, such that the reference force is applied to the fingertip. Given that the force ratio for the distal phalanx is $w_3$, the feedforward term could instead be defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_torque_control_reference_with_force_feedback_scaled_feedforward}
    r_{\jnt{I_a}}(t) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{w_3}\frac{r_\jnt{F} r_{\jnt{spindle}}}{K_t}}_{\text{Scaled feedforward}} + \underbrace{K_p (r_{\jnt{F}} - F_{meas}(t)) + K_i \int_{t_0}^t (r_{\jnt{F}} - F_{meas}(\tau)) \, d\tau}_{\text{PI \, controller}}.
\end{equation}

\section{Model reference adaptive control (MRAC) for position control}%
\label{sec:mrac_implementation}

When the hand is not in contact with any objects, we want to control the position of the hand. However, due to the big differences in mass, stiffness and dampening in the hand between patients, it becomes difficult to design a controller that can guarantee good performance for all patients. To address this issue, we will implement a model reference adaptive control (MRAC) scheme, which will allow us to adapt the controller parameters online based on the observed behavior of the system. To be clear, this is mostly just to speed up the tuning process of the controller, and get good enough control for tests when frequently switching between patients. I would however reccommend to use a simpler control approach if the system is to be used several times by the same patient. This will be discussed in more detail in \cref{ch:discussion}.

As discussed in \cref{subsec:mass_spring_model}, because of modelling uncertainties and the fact that we don't measure anything at the hand, the finger model from \cref{sec:finger_model} becomes unobservable. Therefore controlling the angles of the fingers directly becomes infeasible. Instead we will focus on controlling the position of the motor precisely, as it's connected directly to the finger through a cable. For this we will use the MRAC scheme introduced in \cref{subsec:theory_control_mrac}. It calls for the system to be controllable. Using the simplified model of the finger dynamics as a torsional mass-spring-damper system acting on the motor, from \cref{subsec:state_space_model_load}, we can check if the simplified system is controllable.

\subsection{Controllability of the system}%
\label{subsec:controllability}
Based on the state-space representation our system in \cref{subsec:state_space_model_load}, we can check if the system is controllable, as described in \cref{subsec:theory_control_controllability}. The controllability matrix $\mathcal{C}$ for our system is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:model_controllability}
    \mathcal{C} = [\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}].
\end{equation}

Substituting for our specific $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, we get:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 & \frac{K_t}{J_m+J}\\
        \frac{K_t}{J_m+J} & -\frac{K_t (B_m+c)}{(J_m+J)^2}
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Clearly, $\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{C}) = 2$, which is equal to the number of states. Therefore, the simplified system is controllable.

\subsection{MRAC implementation}%
\label{subsec:controller_design_mrac_implementation}

The MRAC scheme that will be implemented is based on \cref{subsec:theory_control_mrac}. In our physical model of the system, we will use the finger model developed in \cref{sec:finger_model}, connected to the brushed DC motor as a load, through a cable connected to a whiffle tree to spread the force to all three phalanges. From the motor side, the dynamics can be described through the state-space representation:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_mrac_system_dynamics}
    \begin{aligned}
        \underbrace{
        \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \begin{bmatrix}
            \theta\\
            \omega
        \end{bmatrix}}_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
            0 & 1 \\
            0 & -\frac{B_m}{J_m}
        \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}}
        \underbrace{
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \theta\\
            \omega
        \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
            0 \\
            \frac{K_t}{J_m}
        \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{B}} \underbrace{I_a}_{u} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
            0 \\
            \frac{\tau_l}{J_m}
        \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{d}},
        \quad y(t) = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
            1 & 0
        \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{C}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
            \theta\\
            \omega
        \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\theta$ is the angle of the motor, $\omega$ is the angular velocity of the motor, $I_a$ is the armature current, and $\tau_l$ is the load torque applied to the motor by the finger. The output $y(t)$ is the angle of the motor, which we want to control. The MRAC scheme calls for a reference model, which the system should follow as closely as possible. Since our system is a second-order system, we will use a second-order reference model, in the standard form:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_mrac_reference_model}
    \underbrace{
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \begin{bmatrix}
        \theta_m\\
        \omega_m
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}_m} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
        0 & 1 \\
        -\omega_n^2 & -2 \zeta \omega_n
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}_m} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
        \theta_m\\
        \omega_m
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}_m} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
        0 \\
        \omega_n^2
    \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{B}_m} r_{\theta},
\end{equation}

where $\omega_n$ is the natural frequency of the system, $\zeta$ is the damping ratio, and $r_{\theta}$ is the reference angle for the motor that we want the system to follow. The MRAC scheme requires that the reference model is stable. The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}_m$ in \cref{eq:controller_design_mrac_reference_model} are given by:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_mrac_reference_model_eigenvalues}
    \lambda = -\zeta \omega_n \pm \omega_n \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, as long as $\zeta, \omega_n > 0$, the reference model will be stable. Similarly to the torque controller, we'll use the current controller designed in \cref{sec:controller_design_current_controller}, such that we can treat $I_a$ as the control input for the MRAC scheme. We can therefore define the reference current $r_{\jnt{I_a}}$ as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controller_design_mrac_reference_current}
    r_{\jnt{I_a}}(t) = -\mathbf{K}^\intercal(t) \mathbf{x}(t) + L(t) r_{\theta}(t),
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{K}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and $L(t) \in \mathbb{R}$ are the adaptive parameters that will be updated online based on the observed behavior of the system. Defining the tracking error $\mathbf{e}(t)$ as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}(t) = \mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}_m(t).
\end{equation}

Solving for $\mathbf{P}$ for a $\mathbf{Q} =\mathbf{Q}^\intercal > 0$ in the Lyapunov equation:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}_m^\intercal\mathbf{P} + \mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}_m = -\mathbf{Q},
\end{equation}

we can define the adaptive law as:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \dot{\mathbf{K}}(t) &= \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_K \mathbf{x} \mathbf{B}_m^\intercal \mathbf{P} \mathbf{e}, \\
        \dot{L}(t) &= - \gamma_L \mathbf{B}_m^\intercal \mathbf{P} \mathbf{e} r_{\theta}(t),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_K \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ and $\gamma_L \in \mathbb{R}$ are positive definite matrices that determine the adaptation rate of the parameters. 

\subsection{Connecting the motors to the finger through a cable}%

For the simulation of the position controller, we will simulate two motors, one for flexion and one for extension, as is the case in our physical system. The motors will be pulling on each their cables, where force the motors generate gets distributed to the three phalanges through their own whiffle tree, with $w_1 = \frac{1}{2}$ of the force going to the proximal phalanx, $w_2 = \frac{1}{3}$ of the force going to the middle phalanx, and $w_3 = \frac{1}{6}$ of the force going to the distal phalanx. The force application is shown in \cref{fig:controller_design_force_application}. 

\subsubsection{Cable tension}
\label{subsec:cable_tension}

To emulate the cable connecting the motor to the finger, the following constraint is added to the system:

\begin{equation}
    g(\theta, \mathbf{q}) \triangleq r_{spindle} \theta + L_{finger}(\mathbf{q}) - C = 0, \quad \mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix}
        \varphi_{\jnt{MCP}}\\
        \varphi_{\jnt{PIP}}\\
        \varphi_{\jnt{DIP}}
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

where $r_{spindle}$ is the radius of the spindle mounted on the motor, $\theta$ is the angle of the motor, $L_{finger}(\mathbf{q})$ is an estimate for the total length of cable from the finger to the whiffle tree. $C$ is a constant defining the total cable length in the system, which then constraints the angle of the motor and the relative angles of the finger. The estimate of the total cable going from the finger to the whiffle tree is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    L_{finger}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{i = 1}^{3} w_i ||\mathbf{r}_{a, i} - \mathbf{r}_{t, i}||,
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{r}_{a, i}$ and $\mathbf{r}_{t, i}$ are the attachment and target points of the $i$-th phalanx, respectively, shown in \cref{fig:controller_design_force_application}. The distance between the attachment and target points of each phalanx is scaled with the force ratios $w_i$ to get a better estimate of the total length of cable with the whiffle tree. Differentiating the constraint, we get the velocity constraint:

\begin{equation}
    \dot{g}(\omega, \mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) = r_{spindle} \omega + \frac{\partial L_{finger}}{\partial \mathbf{q}} \dot{\mathbf{q}} = 0,
\end{equation}

and further once more we get the acceleration constraint:

\begin{equation}
    \ddot{g}(\dot{\omega}, \mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, \ddot{\mathbf{q}}) = r_{spindle} \dot{\omega} + \frac{\partial L_{finger}}{\partial \mathbf{q}} \ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \dot{\mathbf{q}}^\intercal \frac{\partial^2 L_{finger}}{\partial \mathbf{q}^2} \dot{\mathbf{q}} = 0.   
\end{equation}

To reduce numerical drift, Baumgarte stabilization is added, to combine all the constraints into a single equation:

\begin{equation}
    \label{baumgarte}
    \ddot{g} + 2\alpha \dot{g} + \beta^2 g = 0, \quad \alpha, \beta > 0.
\end{equation}

The final constraint in \cref{baumgarte} is defined separately for the flexion and extension cables. The corresponding Lagrange multipliers are treated as the cable tensions $T_f$ and $T_e$ for the flexion and extension cable, respectively, and are solved simultaneously at each timestep.

Since the cables only can pull on the finger, we further impose that the cable tensions must be non-negative:

\begin{equation}
    T_f, T_e \geq 0.
\end{equation}

To be clear, this implementation of the cable tension is not a perfect emulation of the real system. In reality, the cable is slightly elastic, and the whiffle trees will provide some friction. There will also be more cable in the system. However, considering that the position of the attachement points, target points, and configuration of whiffle trees are not yet decided in the real system, this should provide a good enough approximation to test the position controller, and get a better understanding of the dynamics of the system.

\subsubsection{Updated dynamics with the cable tension}

Given the cable tension $T_x$ prior to the whiffle tree for the $x$-th cable, the $x$-th brushed DC motor experiences a load torque of $\tau_l = r_{spindle} T_x$, giving the updated mechanical dynamics from \cref{eq:motor_model_mechanical_rewrite}:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:motor_dynamics_cable_tension}
    J_m \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\theta_x}{\mathrm{d}t^2}+B_m \frac{\mathrm{d} \theta_x}{\mathrm{d}t} = K_t I_a - r_{spindle} T_x.
\end{equation}

Similarly, the finger experiences a generalized load torque. Since the cable
tension acts along the line from the attachment point $\mathbf{r}_{a,i}$ to the
target point $\mathbf{r}_{t,i}$, the force applied to the $i$-th phalanx is

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{F}_{x, i}
    =
    w_i T_x
    \frac{
        \mathbf{r}_{x,t,i} - \mathbf{r}_{x,a,i}
    }{
        \left\|
            \mathbf{r}_{x,t,i} - \mathbf{r}_{x,a,i}
        \right\|
    }.
\end{equation}

The generalized torque contribution from this force is computed with:

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\tau}_{x, i}
    =
    \frac{\partial (\mathbf{r}_{x,a,i} - \mathbf{r}_{x,t,i})}{\partial \mathbf{q}}^\intercal
    \mathbf{F}_{x, i},
\end{equation}

which leads to the total generalized cable torque on the finger:

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\tau}_{x, cable}
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{3}
    \boldsymbol{\tau}_{x, i}.
\end{equation}

Given that we have two motors pulling on the finger with their own cables, we can calculate the total generalized torque from both the flexion and extension cables on the finger as:

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\tau}_{cable} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \frac{\partial (\mathbf{r}_{f, a,i} - \mathbf{r}_{f, t,i})}{\partial \mathbf{q}}^\intercal
    \mathbf{F}_{f, i} + \sum_{i=1}^{3} \frac{\partial (\mathbf{r}_{e, a,i} - \mathbf{r}_{e, t,i})}{\partial \mathbf{q}}^\intercal
    \mathbf{F}_{e, i}.
\end{equation}

This gives us the updated finger dynamics from \cref{eq:modelling_equations_of_motion}:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:finger_dynamics_cable_tension}
    \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) \ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \left(\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}})+\mathbf{B}\right)\dot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{K} \left(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{q}_{\text{eq}}\right) = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{cable}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Simulation of MRAC scheme with updated dynamics}

\cref{eq:finger_dynamics_cable_tension} is used for simulating the dynamics of the finger, while two sets of \cref{eq:motor_dynamics_cable_tension} are used for simulating the dynamics of the two motors. To connect the models, a cable constraint is added to emulate the cables connecting the motors and the finger, as discussed in \cref{subsec:cable_tension}. The parameters for the motor and finger model are given in \cref{tab:finger_parameters}. The estimation of the motor parameters are done in \cref{subsec:est_motor_params}. The parameters of the finger model are not taken from any literature, but were selected empirically through repeated simulations of the unloaded finger to obtain realistic motion. 

Both cables are initialized to be under tension when the finger is in a straight position, i.e. when $\varphi_{\jnt{MCP}} = \varphi_{\jnt{PIP}} = \varphi_{\jnt{DIP}} = 0$. The flexion motor is initialized to $\theta_f(0) = \SI{0}{\radian}$, while the extension motor is initialized to $\theta_e(0) = \frac{\pi}{2}\si{\radian}$. To make sure the motors aren't working against each other, they receive an opposite reference angle. This ensures that as one motor pulls on the finger, increasing the tension in its cable, the other motor will be rotating in the opposite direction, decreasing the tension in its cable. When the motors are decreasing the tension in their cables, the motor would experience $\tau_l \approx \SI{0}{\newton\metre}$. Therefore the dynamics would change, throwing off the adaptive gains. To account for this, we switch to a pretuned (PT) controller, tuned for a unloaded motor. At the same time we turn off the adaptive law. Once the motor recieves a reference that will increase the tension in the cable, we switch back to the adaptive controller, and turn on the adaptive law. The pretuned controller is tuned through the exact same adaptive controller, just with an unloaded motor for $\SI{100}{\second}$. When the pretuned controller is active, these final values of $\mathbf{K}$ and $L$ are used instead of the adaptive gains. The reference angles for the flexion motor and the extension motor is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        r_{\theta_f}(t) &= \begin{cases}
            \frac{\pi}{2} & \text{if } \sin(\frac{6}{10}\pi\,\theta_f(t)) > 0 \\
            0 & \text{else}
        \end{cases} \\
        r_{\theta_e}(t) &= \theta_e(0) - r_{\theta_f}(t).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The reference models parameters from \cref{eq:controller_design_mrac_reference_model} are set to $\omega_n = 8$ and $\zeta = 1$. $\mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix}
    10 & 0 \\
    0 & 1
\end{bmatrix} \implies \mathbf{P} \approx \begin{bmatrix}
    3.56 & 0.078 \\
    0.078 & 0.036
\end{bmatrix}$. The adaptive gain matrices are set to $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_K = \begin{bmatrix}
    3 & 0 \\
    0 & 0.5
\end{bmatrix}$ and $\gamma_L = 2$. The adaptive gains are initalized to $\mathbf{K}(0) = \begin{bmatrix}
    0.0 \\
    0.0
\end{bmatrix}$ and $L(0) = 1$.

\subsection{Evaluation of MRAC scheme}
\label{subsec:mrac_evaluation}
\cref{fig:mrac_simulation_results_10_seconds} shows a simulation of the MRAC controller over $\SI{10}{\second}$. While the MRAC scheme is active, indicated in orange, the motor angles $\theta_f$ and $\theta_e$ initially oscillate around the reference model. Over time, however, the adaptive gains $\mathbf{K}$ and $L$ change to values that allow the system to track the reference model more closely, and the oscillations become less prominent. While the pretuned controller is active, indicated in grey, the motor tracks the reference model quite closely. Interestingly it seems to have a slightly faster response than the reference model. This might be due some inconsistensies with the cable tensioning implementation.

Continuing the simulation to $\SI{100}{\second}$, \cref{fig:mrac_gains_100_seconds} shows the evolution of the adaptive gains, aswell as the error between the motor angles and their respective reference model angles. \cref{fig:mrac_simulation_results_90_seconds} shows the simulation results for $\SI{10}{\second}$ after $\SI{90}{\second}$. Looking at \cref{fig:mrac_gains_100_seconds}, after $\sim \SI{10}{\second}$, $K_2$ seem to converge to a certain value, while $K_1$ and $L$ eventually keeps growing together, almost logarithmically. The continued growth of $K_1$ and $L$ might be due to the fact that the system is trying to find gains that can track the reference model better, but is limited by the maximum current that the motor can provide. Looking at the current commands going to the motors in \cref{fig:mrac_simulation_results_90_seconds}, it can be observed that they are clipped by the maximum stall current of $\SI{4.9}{\ampere}$. Looking back at \cref{fig:mrac_gains_100_seconds}, it's clear that the error between the motor angles and their respective reference model decrease over time as the adaptive gains update. However they don't go to zero, but end up with some steady-state error, though quite small. This tells us that the adaptive controller gains at some point reach a local optimum. Therefore, to combat the ever increasing gains, we may want to switch to a fixed-gain controller once the gains have converged. 

\cref{fig:fixed_gains_mrac} shows a simulation over $\SI{10}{\second}$ using the adaptive gains found in \cref{fig:mrac_gains_100_seconds} after $\SI{100}{\second}$, but with the gains fixed instead of adaptive. Here we can see that the motors are able to track their reference angles rather well, only with a slight error in the transition to a new reference angle. Hence we may conclude that the MRAC scheme could prove useful in quickly tuning a position controller, when frequently switching between patients.
